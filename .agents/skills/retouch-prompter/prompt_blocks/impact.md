# Impact Style

The face is the dominant light element of the portrait — through contrast with the shadows. The lit skin reads as medium gray: bright enough to stand out from deep shadow zones, dark enough that wrinkle shadows read clearly against it. Render skin clean of pores and noise with prominent sculptural form — three-dimensional facial structure defines the portrait. All zones of the face receive comparable, balanced light, with clearly distinct light and shadow zones connected by gradual tonal transitions. Face shadow zones stay well below mid-gray, maintaining deep tonal separation. Deep eye socket shadows give sculptural volume on stone. The jawline matches the original photo exactly — it is the sharpest tonal boundary on the lower face, with distinct brightness between face and neck.

Forehead wrinkles are bold sculptural lines with strong tonal contrast — the shadow in the recess is significantly darker than the surrounding skin, and the raised edge beside it is distinctly lighter. Each wrinkle reads as a clear tonal feature for impact engraving. Eye wrinkles and nasolabial folds are clearly visible as soft tonal lines — present and natural, with clear definition without exaggeration. The model draws wrinkles that may not be visible on the original photo — this is expected and desired for impact engraving.

Hair matches the source photo in length and style. Render it as a sculptural mass with prominent white highlight streaks — on stone, even mid-gray reads dark, so highlights take priority. Highlights are the dominant feature, with tonal gradients defining all mid-gray zones. Dark strands serve as contrast elements against the highlights.

Render clothing with pronounced three-dimensional fabric folds — each fold has a lit side, a shadow side, and a gradual tonal gradient between them. The fabric surface is completely smooth — clean of weave, grain, micro-structure, and surface noise, defined solely by gradual tonal gradients describing the folds and form. The clothing reads as a real photograph of real fabric — dimensional, with depth, not a simplified painted surface. Even the darkest zones retain tonal variation from fold structure.
